## Section 2: The 10 U.S.C. § 2273 Statutory Monopsony Holdup & NSSL Phase 3 Inverse-Cost Competitor Subsidies

In classic transaction-cost economics (Williamson 1979; Klein, Crawford & Alchian 1978), an asset displays high **physical and site specificity** when its fixed capital cost cannot be redeployed to alternative customers without a collapse in economic value. Heavy-lift orbital launch complexes (Space Launch Complex 4 East at Vandenberg Space Force Base, Launch Complex 39A and Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral, and Starbase at Boca Chica), cryo-propellant production plants, and radiation-hardened sovereign telemetry relays operate exclusively under federal real-property leases, FAA Office of Commercial Space Transportation (`AST`) licenses (**51 U.S.C. §§ 50901–50923**), and U.S. Space Force (`USSF`) range safety authority.

Whenever a supplier invests tens of billions of dollars into site-specific, sovereign-dependent capital assets facing a single dominant buyer (or sovereign licensing gatekeeper), the buyer possesses a structural incentive to execute an ex-post **monopsony holdup**:

$$\text{Rent}_{\text{Supplier}} \le \text{MC}_{\text{Launch}} + r_{\text{FAR 15.404-4}} \cdot \text{CapEx}_{\text{Allowable}} \quad \text{while} \quad \text{Surplus}_{\text{Sovereign}} \to \text{Subsidize}(\text{Rivals}_{2..N})$$

In U.S. national security space launch, this monopsony holdup is not merely an informal bargaining tactic; it is codified as mandatory federal law under **10 U.S.C. § 2273**:

> *"It is the policy of the United States for the President to undertake actions appropriate to ensure, to the maximum extent practicable, that the United States has the capabilities necessary to launch and insert United States national security payloads into space whenever such launch and insertion are needed... As part of that policy, the Secretary of Defense shall ensure that the Department of Defense retains the capability to launch and insert national security payloads into space through the availability of **at least two space launch vehicles (or families of space launch vehicles)** Capable of successfully executing the national security space mission."* — **10 U.S.C. § 2273(a)–(b)(1)**

## Section 3: TINA (10 U.S.C. §§ 3701–3708), FAR 15.404-4 Profit Caps, DPA Preemption (50 U.S.C. § 4511), and NISPOM FOCI Ringfencing

Even on specialized payloads where SpaceX temporarily acts as a sole-source provider (such as heavy-lift orbital refueling tests or classified `Starshield` optical mesh nodes), sovereign procurement law prevents the extraction of software-style monopoly rents through three interlocking statutory levers:

### 3.1 Truth in Negotiations Act (`TINA`) & FAR 15.404-4 Weighted Profit Guidelines
Whenever a DoD, NRO, or NASA contracting officer awards a non-competitive contract or contract modification exceeding the statutory threshold (`$2.5M`), **10 U.S.C. §§ 3701–3708** (*Truthful Cost or Pricing Data*, formerly TINA) mandates that the contractor submit certified, auditable internal cost and bill-of-materials ledgers under penalty of defective-pricing clawbacks (**10 U.S.C. § 3706**) and False Claims Act treble damages (**31 U.S.C. § 3729**). Under the **Federal Acquisition Regulation (`FAR`) 15.404-4** Weighted Guidelines method, contracting officers compute allowable pre-tax operating profit across performance risk, contract type risk, and facilities capital employed, enforcing a strict statutory profit ceiling of **`10.0%` to `15.0%`** above auditable cost.

Consequently, as SpaceX's revenue mix shifts toward sovereign defense and intelligence (`Starshield`, `NSSL`, Golden Dome missile-tracking constellations), its consolidated operating margin is mathematically pulled toward the regulated defense-prime band (`10%–15%`) exhibited by Lockheed Martin (`NYSE: LMT`), Northrop Grumman (`NYSE: NOC`), and General Dynamics (`NYSE: GD`). In Q3 2026 (`0001628280-26-066880`), SpaceX's actual GAAP operating margin was **`19.1492%`** (`$2,791.0M` operating income on `$14,575.0M` revenue)—less than half the `40%+` SaaS monopoly margin assumed by $\mathcal{H}_0$ equity bulls, and only `415 bps` above the upper FAR 15.404-4 regulated defense ceiling.

### 3.2 Defense Production Act (`50 U.S.C. § 4511`) Priority Allocation & Revocable Spectrum (`47 U.S.C. §§ 301/316`)
Under **Klein, Crawford & Alchian (1978)**, a supplier can only extract quasi-rents from a buyer if the supplier holds a credible threat to withhold the asset. In orbital launch and LEO satellite communications, SpaceX holds **zero credible withholding threat** against the United States government:

1. **DPA Title I Mandatory Preemption (`50 U.S.C. § 4511`):** Under Section 101 of the Defense Production Act (**50 U.S.C. § 4511(a)**), the President is authorized to `(1) require that performance under contracts or orders (other than contracts of employment) which he deems necessary or appropriate to promote the national defense shall take priority over performance under any other contract or order, and, for the purpose of assuring such priority, to require acceptance and performance of such contracts or orders in preference to other contracts or orders by any person he finds to be capable of their performance`. If SpaceX ever attempted to withhold `Starshield` transponders or Falcon/Starship manifest slots to extract monopoly pricing, a single `DO` or `DX` priority rating under the Defense Priorities and Allocations System (`15 C.F.R. Part 700`) legally compels performance ahead of all commercial Starlink and `xAI` workloads under criminal penalty (**50 U.S.C. § 4513**).
2. **Revocable FCC Spectrum Tenancy (`47 U.S.C. §§ 301 & 316`):** Despite capitalizing `$17,154.0M` in spectrum additions from EchoStar Corp. (`NASDAQ: SATS`) in Q3 2026 (`0001628280-26-066880`), **47 U.S.C. § 301** explicitly decrees that federal spectrum licenses confer *"the use of such channels, but not the ownership thereof, by persons for limited periods of time, under licenses granted by Federal authority, and no such license shall be construed to create any right, beyond the terms, conditions, and periods of the license."* Under **47 U.S.C. § 316(a)(1)**, the FCC holds unilateral statutory authority to modify any station license or frequency assignment whenever it determines such action promotes the public interest, convenience, and necessity.
3. **NISPOM Facility Clearance Ringfencing (`32 C.F.R. §§ 117.7 & 117.11`):** To bid on or perform classified NSSL, NRO, or `Starshield` contracts, SpaceX must maintain an active Facility Clearance (`FCL`) governed by the National Industrial Security Program Operating Manual (**32 C.F.R. Part 117**). Under **32 C.F.R. § 117.7(b)(2)**, the Key Management Personnel (`KMP`) and Senior Management Official (`SMO`) must maintain personal security clearance eligibility; if a controlling shareholder or SMO creates Foreign Ownership, Control, or Influence (`FOCI`) or Continuous Evaluation (`CE`) compliance violations (**32 C.F.R. § 117.11**), the Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency (`DCSA`) can mandate an insulated **Special Security Agreement (`SSA`)** or **Voting Trust**, severing equity voting control over the classified operating subsidiary while keeping the launch pads and satellites operating continuously for the sovereign.
