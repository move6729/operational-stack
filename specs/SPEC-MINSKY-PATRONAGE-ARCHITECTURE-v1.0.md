## 3. The 1969 *Perceptrons* Epistemic Boycott & The 1973 Lighthill External Audit

### 3.1 The Competing Hardware Baseline: Frank Rosenblatt's Mark I Perceptron (1957–1960)

During the late 1950s and 1960s, the primary competitor to MIT's discrete symbolic-logic program for ONR and Rome Air Development Center (RADC) funding was his Bronx High School of Science classmate Frank Rosenblatt at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory. Supported by ONR, Rosenblatt constructed the **Mark I Perceptron** (`1957–1960`): a physical parallel hardware classifier using a **`20 x 20 = 400`** cadmium-sulfide photocell sensory array wired to motorized potentiometers that updated continuous synaptic weights $w_i$ via error-driven reinforcement.

Unlike MIT's hand-coded LISP toy worlds, Rosenblatt's architecture operated directly on noisy analog physical light fields and satisfied Landauer's (1961) physical-computation realism by mapping learning to continuous state-space trajectories rather than human-authored predicate rules.

### 3.2 *Perceptrons* (1969): From Local Order-Restricted Theorems to Global Epistemic Boycott

In 1969, Minsky and Seymour Papert published *Perceptrons: An Introduction to Computational Geometry* (MIT Press). The mathematical core of the volume proved two legitimate geometric bounds on **single-layer, order-restricted** threshold networks:

1. **Theorem 3.1.1 (Parity Predicate Invariance):** A single-layer perceptron can compute the boolean parity predicate $\text{Parity}(X)$ over a retina $R$ of $|R|$ pixels only if the maximum support size (order $k$) of at least one feature predicate $\varphi_i$ satisfies:
   $$k_{\text{Parity}} = |R|$$
   Consequently, any order-restricted single-layer perceptron with $k < |R|$ cannot compute `XOR` ($|R| = 2, k = 1$) or general parity.
2. **Theorem 5.5 (Topological Connectedness):** Any single-layer perceptron computing topological connectedness over a discrete grid $R$ requires feature predicates whose order grows at least as:
   $$k_{\text{Connectedness}} = \Omega\left(|R|^{1/2}\right)$$

Both theorems hold strictly for **depth-1 linear threshold networks** where the feature layer $\Phi$ is fixed and non-trainable. However, in Chapter 13 (*Perceptrons and Pattern Recognition*, pp. 231–232), Minsky and Papert appended an unproven verbal extrapolation regarding multi-layer networks:

> *"The perceptron has shown itself worthy of study despite (and even because of!) its severe limitations. It has many features to attract attention: its linearity; its intriguing learning theorem; its clear paradigmatic simplicity as a kind of parallel computation. There is no reason to suppose that any of these virtues carry over to the many-layered version. Nevertheless, we consider it to be an important research problem to elucidate (or reject) our intuitive judgment that the extension is sterile."*

Notice the rhetorical inversion: a mathematical proof regarding a 1-layer linear separator was paired with an *"intuitive judgment that the extension [to multi-layer networks] is sterile."* Combined with Frank Rosenblatt's sudden death in a Chesapeake Bay boating accident on July 11, 1971 (his 43rd birthday) and the passage of the **Mansfield Amendment** (**Pub. As documented by Olson (1965), when a public subsidy pool contracts or faces statutory tightening, an entrenched insider coalition maximizes its survival by disqualifying rival paradigms. Federal ONR and DARPA funding for connectionist neural networks was effectively zeroed out for seventeen years until Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams formalized multi-layer backpropagation in 1986.
