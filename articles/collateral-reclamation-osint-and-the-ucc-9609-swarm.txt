## 1. State the Case: How Defaulting Commercial Debtors Weaponize the Gated-Curtilage Catch-22

Suppose an equipment lessor or distressed-credit fund holds a perfected first-priority security interest (`U.C.C. § 9-501`) in a $\$450,000$ Caterpillar hydraulic excavator, a $\$1.2\text{ million}$ modular liquid-cooled GPU inference container, or a fleet of heavy commercial rolling stock. The borrower stops paying, dissolves its operating company, and parks the machinery behind a locked steel gate on a 40-acre rural parcel titled to a newly formed Wyoming or Delaware manager-managed LLC.

On paper, Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code gives the secured creditor sweeping remedies. Under `U.C.C. § 9-609(a)`, after default, a secured party "(1) may take possession of the collateral; and (2) without removal, may render equipment unusable and dispose of collateral on a debtor's premises under Section 9-610." Under `U.C.C. § 9-609(b)`, the creditor may proceed "(1) pursuant to judicial process; or (2) without judicial process, if it proceeds without breach of the peace."

In the physical world, however, the defaulting debtor has trapped the creditor inside the **Gated-Curtilage Catch-22**:

1. **You cannot cut the lock via self-help (`U.C.C. § 9-609(b)(2)`):** While a repossessor may lawfully hook a vehicle from an unenclosed driveway at 4:30 a.m. (*Williams v. Ford Motor Credit Co.*, 674 F.2d 717, 719–20 (8th Cir. 1982)) or a public parking lot (*Clarin v. Minnesota Repossessors, Inc.*, 198 F.3d 661, 665 (8th Cir. 1999)), breaking a padlock, hopping a perimeter fence, or entering enclosed curtilage is a textbook **breach of the peace** (*Giles v. First Virginia Credit Services, Inc.*, 560 S.E.2d 557 (N.C. Ct. App. 2002)). Crossing that line converts the creditor from a lienholder into a tortfeasor, and it does something worse than create tort exposure: it is a *failure to proceed in accordance with Part 6*, which arms `U.C.C. § 9-626(a)(3)–(4)`. Once the debtor places compliance in issue, the burden flips to the secured party under `§ 9-626(a)(2)`, and if it fails, `§ 9-626(a)(4)` **presumes** that a complying disposition would have realized the full secured obligation plus expenses and attorney's fees. The deficiency under `§ 9-626(a)(3)` then collapses to $\$0$. On a $\$650,000$ note secured by a $\$450,000$ excavator, the creditor that cuts one padlock forfeits a $\$200,000$ deficiency judgment outright — before a single dollar of `U.C.C. § 9-625(b)` noncompliance damages ("any loss caused by a failure to comply with this article," including the debtor's alternative-financing loss), common-law conversion preserved by `U.C.C. § 1-103(b)`, or punitive damages.

   Note what is *not* on that list: the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act. `15 U.S.C. § 1692a(5)` confines "debt" to obligations "primarily for personal, family, or household purposes." Commercial yellow-iron, GPU containers, hulls, and Class 8 rolling stock sit entirely outside it. `15 U.S.C. §§ 1692f(6), 1692k` bite only where the pool is commingled with consumer auto or household-goods paper — and the commercial `§§ 9-625(b), 9-626(a)(3)–(4)` remedy they displace is larger, not smaller.
2. **You cannot get a Sheriff lock-break writ without proving the asset is already inside (`N.Y. CPLR § 7102` / `Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 512.010`):** When self-help is blocked by a locked gate, the creditor must seek an ex parte Order of Seizure or Writ of Possession under `Fed. R. Civ. P. 64`, `N.Y. CPLR § 7102(d)(1)`, or `Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 512.010`. A Sheriff or U.S. Marshal has full statutory authority to cut locks and enter private enclosures—but *only* if the creditor's affidavit first establishes probable cause that the specific serialized chattel is currently inside that exact enclosure. As `N.Y. CPLR § 7102(c)(5)` explicitly requires, the supporting affidavit must state "the place where the chattel is located and facts sufficient to establish probable cause to believe that the chattel is located at that place." `Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 512.010(b)(4)` identically requires "a showing that there is probable cause to believe that such property is located there."

   The lock-break power itself is conferred downstream and only conditionally. `N.Y. CPLR § 7102(d)(1)` lets the court grant an order "including, **if the court so directs, a provision that, if the chattel is not delivered to the sheriff, he may break open, enter and search for the chattel in the place specified in the affidavit**" — two conditions (judicial direction *plus* non-delivery), scoped to the *specified* place. California's levy authority is `Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 514.010(c)`, not § 512.010: the levying officer must first "announce his identity, purpose, and authority," and only "[i]f the property is not voluntarily delivered" may he "cause any building or enclosure where the property may be located to be broken open in such a manner as he reasonably believes will cause the least damage" — with a mandatory stand-down and return to the court where entry "will involve a substantial risk of death or serious bodily harm to any person." Then `§ 514.010(d)` snaps the trap shut: "Nothing in this section authorizes the levying officer to enter or search any private place **not specified in the writ of possession**." Both states also bond the remedy — `Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 515.010(a)` ("the court shall not issue a temporary restraining order or a writ of possession until the plaintiff has filed an undertaking," "not less than twice the value of the defendant's interest in the property") and `N.Y. CPLR § 7102(e)` (surety "bound in a specified amount, not less than twice the value of the chattel stated in the plaintiff's affidavit") — the constitutional floor set by *Fuentes v. Shevin*, 407 U.S. 67 (1972) and *Mitchell v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 416 U.S. 600 (1974). Get the coordinates wrong and the plaintiff pays on that bond.

Here lies the Catch-22: **you cannot enter the gated parcel to verify the collateral is inside without a writ, and you cannot obtain the writ without first verifying the collateral is inside.** Because legacy skip-tracers and ground investigators are stopped at the perimeter fence along the rural highway, defaulted equipment notes routinely trade at $8\text{ cents on the dollar}$ ($V_{\text{Distressed}} = \$40,000$ on a $\$500,000$ face note) while the underlying machinery continues generating illicit cash flow for the debtor's nominee cutout.

Part 107`):** By federal statute, "[t]he United States Government has exclusive sovereignty of airspace of the United States" (`49 U.S.C. § 40103(a)(1)`), and "[a] citizen of the United States has a public right of transit through the navigable airspace" (`§ 40103(a)(2)`), exercised subject to the FAA Administrator's `§ 40103(b)(1)–(2)` airspace-use assignments and regulations "protecting individuals and property on the ground." In *Dow Chemical Co. v. United States*, 476 U.S. 227, 236–39 (1986), Chief Justice Burger held for the Supreme Court that aerial photography of a fenced 2,000-acre industrial complex from navigable airspace using a precision commercial mapping camera is neither a Fourth Amendment search nor a common-law trespass:
   > "The open areas of an industrial plant complex such as petitioner's are not analogous to the 'curtilage' of a dwelling, which is entitled to protection as a place where the occupants have a reasonable and legitimate expectation of privacy that society is prepared to accept... For purposes of aerial surveillance, the open areas of an industrial complex are more comparable to an 'open field' in which an individual may not legitimately demand privacy."

   Three years later, in *Florida v. Riley*, 488 U.S. 445, 449–51 (1989), the Supreme Court extended that rule to observations from 400 feet AGL—the exact upper ceiling of `14 C.F.R. Part 107` small unmanned aircraft operations (`14 C.F.R. § 107.51(b)`).

   **Read the holding's scope carefully.** *Dow Chemical* and *Riley* are **state-action** cases. They tell you what the *government* may do, and they are why the Sheriff executing your Order of Seizure survives a vacatur motion and why your affidavit evidence is not suppressed. They do not license a *private* hunter node. The Fourth Amendment does not constrain private actors at all, so the binding boundary for a commercial OSINT operator is a different body of law entirely:
   - **State sUAS surveillance statutes.** `Tex. Gov't Code § 423.003(a)` makes it a Class C misdemeanor to "use[] an unmanned aircraft to capture an image of an individual or **privately owned real property** in this state with the intent to conduct surveillance on the individual or property captured in the image." There is no commercial-property exception. `§ 423.004` then makes each image *possessed* a separate Class C misdemeanor and each image *disclosed, displayed, distributed, or otherwise used* a separate Class B misdemeanor — and filing a frame as an affidavit exhibit is unmistakably a "use." `§ 423.006` adds a civil action. A five-parcel Texas sortie that produces forty affidavit-quality frames is not one misdemeanor; it is dozens.
   - **Common-law aerial trespass.** *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 264 (1946) and `Restatement (Second) of Torts § 159` ("[a]n entry upon the possession of land of another may be made by the flight of an aircraft above the surface") fix the private-actor boundary in the immediate reaches of the surface.
   - **California's narrower trap.** `Cal. Civ. Code § 1708.8(a)–(b)` looks broad — physical invasion of privacy expressly covers entering "the airspace above the land of another," and constructive invasion covers capture "through the use of any device, technology, or other instrument." But by its terms it protects only a plaintiff "engaging in a **personal or familial** activity." Commercial equipment storage is not that. What § 1708.8 does capture is incidental imaging of *people* on the parcel, which the sensor stack must suppress at capture.

   The operational consequence is a hard **jurisdictional gate**: where the forum state criminalizes sUAS capture of privately owned real property, the sUAS modality is disabled and serialized identification is obtained instead from commercial orbital SAR/EO tasking, public-road right-of-way ground optics at or beyond the parcel boundary, or manned aircraft under `14 C.F.R. Part 91`. Slower; still zero trespass.
