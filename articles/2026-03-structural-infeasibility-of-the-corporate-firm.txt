I. THE COASEAN FOUNDATION AND ITS COLLAPSE

In 1937, Ronald Coase explained why corporate firms exist: internal administrative hierarchies reduce the transaction costs of discovering prices, drafting contracts, and coordinating labor compared to open market bargaining. For nearly a century, the corporate firm—a centralized human swarm anchored to a single legal entity and governed by top-down Command and Control (C2)—served as the dominant structural vessel for economic capital.

That economic justification has completely inverted.

With local quantized silicon (`LMCI-v1.0`), open-weights model inference, schema-enforced artifact execution, and zero-rent peer-to-peer task coordination (`ATN-v1.0`), the transaction costs of decentralized coordination have fallen to near zero ($\approx \$0.00$ OpEx). Conversely, the corporate firm now carries massive structural friction: management overhead, executive bloat, corporate governance taxes, middleman platform fees, and linear legal liability.

The Coasean boundary has flipped inside out: it is now strictly more expensive to coordinate economic activity inside a corporate hierarchy than across an open zero-rent edge federation.
