I. THE UNACKNOWLEDGED LEGAL EXPOSURE OF CENTRALIZED DATA HARVESTING

A dominant assumption across the artificial intelligence industry asserts that centralized cloud infrastructure is the most efficient and legally defensible mechanism for web-scale data collection. Leading AI labs—including OpenAI, Anthropic, Perplexity, and their commercial data vendors—have deployed massive cloud-hosted scraper fleets to index public web data for model training and real-time retrieval.

To justify this posture, corporate legal counsel relies heavily on landmark judicial precedents, specifically the Supreme Court's ruling in Van Buren v. United States (141 S. Ct. 1645) and the Ninth Circuit's decision in hiQ Labs, Inc. v. LinkedIn Corp. (31 F.4th 1180). These decisions established a critical principle of digital law: accessing unauthenticated, publicly available web data does not violate the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA 18 U.S.C. Section 1030).

However, a severe mechanistic mismatch exists between the high-level legal theory cited by lab executives and the low-level network mechanics actually executed by their automated scraping infrastructure. In daily practice, the centralized Command-and-Control (C2) scraping architectures operated by OpenAI, Anthropic, and Perplexity routinely cross statutory boundaries, exposing these companies—and their individual executives—to systemic civil and criminal liability under CFAA Section 1030 and federal conspiracy statutes (18 U.S.C. Section 371).

II. THREE CRITICAL CFAA STATUTORY VIOLATIONS IN CENTRALIZED SCRAPING

1. ACTIVE IDENTITY OBFUSCATION AND TPM CIRCUMVENTION

While Van Buren and hiQ protect the passive reading of unauthenticated public text, they explicitly do not authorize the active circumvention of technological protection measures (TPMs) erected by system administrators. When webmasters detect excessive scraping traffic, they routinely deploy rate limits, Cloudflare or Imperva Web Application Firewalls (WAFs), CAPTCHA challenges, and explicit IP-range blocks.

To overcome these technical barriers, central AI labs and commercial proxy aggregators route their scraping traffic through massive residential proxy networks, while relying on third-party CAPTCHA-solving services and solver farms to bypass access verification gates. By continuously cycling outbound requests through millions of leased or SDK-monetized consumer IP addresses, actively spoofing browser User-Agent headers, and paying third-party vendors to automatically solve or bypass CAPTCHA challenges, these scrapers deliberately obfuscate their identity to evade WAF rate limits and disguise their origin. Under established CFAA case law, actively concealing origin infrastructure and funding automated CAPTCHA bypass to breach security barriers converts lawful public reading into intentional, deceitful circumvention of technological access controls, directly triggering Section 1030(a)(2) and Section 1030(a)(5) liability. This active obfuscation destroys good-faith fair-use defenses and establishes willful criminal intent under 18 U.S.C. Section 371.

2. INTENTIONAL SYSTEM IMPAIRMENT AND TRAFFIC SWARMS (SECTION 1030(a)(5)(A))

Section 1030(a)(5)(A) of the CFAA prohibits knowingly transmitting code or commands that intentionally cause damage without authorization to a protected computer. Section 1030(e)(8) defines "damage" as any impairment to the integrity or availability of data, a system, or information.

When centralized cloud orchestrators dispatch thousands of uncoordinated worker threads against independent web publishers, the resulting request spikes frequently exhaust target server CPU capacity, saturate network bandwidth, and trigger HTTP 503 service outages. Under federal law, generating request volumes that impair target system availability constitutes statutory damage. The fact that the underlying intent was model training rather than a malicious Denial of Service (DoS) attack is legally irrelevant under Section 1030(a)(5)(A).

3. COMMAND-AND-CONTROL (C2) BOTNET VICARIOUS LIABILITY

Centralized scraping architecture relies on a centralized master brain—a Command-and-Control (C2) server matrix that remotely directs distributed worker nodes. Under Section 1030(g), maintaining direct C2 oversight over remote execution agents creates direct vicarious liability for the central operator. Because lab executives and lead infrastructure engineers knowingly fund, architect, and direct these centralized proxy swarms to bypass technical barriers, they face direct exposure to corporate civil claims and criminal conspiracy charges.
