## 1. Two Arms of the Same Empire

Look at the two most celebrated "benefit of humanity" business models of the decade and you will find they are not two models at all. They are one machine with two arms.

Arm one is the charitable equity conversion. An AI laboratory incorporates as a `26 U.S.C. § 501(c)(3)` public charity. It pledges, in a December 2015 founding charter, to publish its weights openly "for the benefit of humanity." Under that pledge it recruits the world's best researchers at below-market compensation, collects tax-deductible donations, and borrows the moral authority of philanthropy. Then, over a sequence of restructurings — capped-profit subsidiary in 2019, for-profit public benefit corporation in 2025 — it migrates the core IP and the workforce into a vehicle valued at `$157.0B` in its October 2024 primary round, leaving the original charity with an approximately `25.0%` residual stake per the issuer's own October 2025 recapitalization disclosure.

Arm two is the biometric iris-scan company scrip. The same technocratic UBI empire ships chrome orbs to the global south, scans the irises of impoverished citizens, and pays each scan in a low-float token. Insiders and financial investors hold approximately `25.0%` of the fully diluted supply per the issuer's published launch allocation; the initial circulating float at the July 2023 launch was approximately `1.5%`. The citizen receives a grant denominated in a liability of the very corporation that now holds her face.

Neither arm announces itself as enclosure. Arm one calls itself a restructuring; arm two calls itself an enrollment. That is the difficulty, and that is why this audit is written as arithmetic rather than rhetoric: the numbers do not care what the brochures say.

Stafford Beer's POSIWID — *the purpose of a system is what it does* — collapses the two arms into one sentence: a tax-advantaged trust shell accumulates public legitimacy, donated labor, and donated capital, and a later structural conversion moves the accumulated value onto a private cap table while the original beneficiaries receive either a minority equity stub or an irrevocably tethered scrip grant.

The two arms also share a customer-acquisition strategy: moral urgency. Arm one recruits researchers with the argument that concentration of capability is necessary to keep it safe and beneficial; arm two recruits scan-subjects with the argument that the alternative is being left behind by the future. In both cases the person being recruited supplies the raw material of their own enclosure — labor in the first case, biometric entropy in the second — and in both cases the recruitment pitch is recorded, in charters and in whitepapers, where it can later be quoted.

## 2. Vector A — The Charitable Bait-and-Switch, in Four Lines of Arithmetic

The conversion is not a mystery. It is four lines of arithmetic:

```
THE CHARITABLE EQUITY CONVERSION (Vector A)

 +-----------------------------+          +--------------------------------+
 | 501(c)(3) PUBLIC CHARITY    |  IP +    | FOR-PROFIT PBC                 |
 | open-source pledge (2015)   | labor +  | recapitalized at               |
 | donated labor, deductible   |--------> | V_conv = $157.0B               |
 | capital, elite researchers  | capital  | charity residual s = 0.25      |
 +-----------------------------+          | private block (1 - s) = 0.75   |
                                          +--------------------------------+

 D_inurement = V_conv * (s*_charity - s_charity)
             = 157.0 * (1.0 - 0.25)
             = $117.75B of charitable trust value migrated to private holders
```

Read the diagram slowly, because the entire case is inside it. The charity was not a minority investor that happened to do well; it was the *trust* — the entity whose exemption, whose donated labor, and whose donated capital built the asset. Under the private inurement ban of `26 U.S.C. § 501(c)(3)` and Treas. Reg. § 1.501(c)(3)-1(d)(1)(ii), no part of a charity's net earnings may inure to the benefit of any private shareholder or individual. The fiduciary fair share of the charitable purpose is therefore not a negotiation: `s*_charity = 1.0`. When the conversion leaves the charity holding `0.25` of a `$157.0B` enterprise, the difference — `$117.75B` — is the excess benefit, defined with statutory precision by `26 U.S.C. § 4958(c)(1)(A)` as the value of the economic benefit provided minus the consideration received.

The structural pattern is pleaded in *Musk v. Altman et al.*, N.D. Cal. Case No. 4:24-cv-04722, where the founding-agreement and charitable-purpose claims are advanced as pleaded. This article does not try those claims; it prices them.

One term in the statute deserves emphasis: *consideration received*. The `§ 4958(c)(1)(A)` excess benefit is not the value that moved; it is the value that moved **net of what came back**. If the for-profit vehicle had paid the charity fair value for the IP, the license revenue, and the franchise of the mission brand, the net excess benefit would collapse toward zero. The conversion did not work that way. The charity's return consideration was the residual stub itself — a minority equity position in the thing it had built, priced by the converter rather than by any adversarial process. That is the structural signature of an excess benefit transaction: the consideration loop closes inside the same boardroom that approved the transfer.

The donated-labor ledger is equally measurable. Elite researchers joined the charity under a public-benefit charter, accepting compensation below what competing laboratories offered precisely because the equity they were building was supposed to belong to humanity. When the conversion repriced that labor into a private cap table, the below-market discount became part of the excess benefit: consideration given by the charity's workforce, consideration received by the private holders. Form 990 compensation disclosures and the cap table together price this leg too.

## 3. The Statutory Counter-Ledger: a 200% Excise and a Constructive Trust

The Internal Revenue Code already contains the counter-weapon, and it is arithmetic too.

```
THE STATUTORY COUNTER-LEDGER

 IRC 4958(a)(1)  initial excise        25%  x  D_inurement
 IRC 4958(b)     second-tier excise   200%  x  D_inurement   (if uncorrected)

 P_4958 = tau_4958 * D_inurement = 2.0 * 117.75 = $235.5B

 Cy pres constructive trust (Cal. Corp. Code 5142, Restatement (Third)
 of Trusts 67, common-law equity):

 G_cy_pres = rho_ct * D_inurement = 1.0 * 117.75 = $117.75B disgorgement
```

Two observations. First, the second-tier `§ 4958(b)` rate of `200%` is not a drafting accident; it is Congress pricing the strategy of taking the benefit and daring the Service to notice. The disqualified persons under `§ 4958(f)` — officers, directors, trustees, anyone in a position to exercise substantial influence — face a `$235.5B` statutory exposure that is exactly double the diversion itself.

Second, the conversion arbitrage reduces to a single scalar. Conversion is the dominant strategy for insiders if and only if the perceived probability of enforcement is below the breakeven:

```
 p* = D_inurement / (P_4958 + G_cy_pres)
    = 117.75 / (235.5 + 117.75)
    = 1 / (tau_4958 + rho_ct)
    = 1/3
    = 0.333333
```

If the enforcement apparatus is perceived to assign less than a one-in-three chance of sustaining an excess-benefit finding, defection pays. If it assigns more, compliance dominates. The entire deterrence question for the largest charitable conversion in history is one fraction. That is the content of Theorem 1 in the canonical spec, and it compiles.

Two statutory mechanics sharpen the point. First, `26 U.S.C. § 4958` also reaches organization managers who knowingly approve an excess benefit, at 10% per transaction — the signature liability that converts a board's restructuring vote into a personal exposure. Second, the `§ 4958` regulations make correction the only escape from the 200% tier: the disqualified person must undo the excess benefit plus interest, which for a converted AI laboratory means either returning the IP to the trust or paying its difference. The statute, in other words, does not merely price the diversion; it specifies the unwinding. Cal. Corp. Code § 5142 supplies the state-law arm of the same unwinding through a breach-of-charitable-trust action, with the *cy pres* constructive trust as the equitable vehicle.

Nor is the second tier the only number on the page. The 25% initial tax under `§ 4958(a)(1)` accrues to the disqualified persons for every year the excess benefit remains uncorrected, stacking beneath the 200% second tier like interest on a margin loan the board took out against the charity's future. The arithmetic of correction is therefore not a penalty; it is a price, and the price rises every year the diversion stands.

No actuarial table prices that leg of the trade at the scan fee. The scan fee is the bait; the identity rail is the asset.

This is what makes the construction a panopticon rather than a product. A payment system you can leave is a service; a payment system built on a secret you can never change is a tether. ## 6. The Company-Scrip UBI Fallacy

Call the thing by its accounting name. A grant paid in a token with a `66.666667x` FDV overhang, a `16.666667x` insider capture ratio, and a redemption rail controlled by the issuer is not universal basic income. It is company scrip — the coal-town store token of the 19th century, reissued on a blockchain and funded with other people's faces.

The kernel-aligned substitute is documented in `AUDIT-2026-UBC-VS-FIAT-UBI.md`: Universal Basic Compute — a dividend paid in capability over public-domain weights, not in a revocable corporate liability. Fiat UBI makes the recipient a creditor of the empire that scanned her; UBC makes her a holder of the means of inference itself. The pattern has a history. In the 19th-century company town, the mine paid in scrip redeemable only at the company store, and the store's prices were set by the same ledger that set the wage. The worker's nominal income rose while his real option set shrank, because every dollar of pay was also a dollar of dependence. The blockchain reissue changes the costume, not the structure: the grant is denominated in the issuer's own liability, redeemable through rails the issuer controls, against an unlock schedule the issuer wrote. The appropriate word is not income. It is enclosure.

Run the purchasing-power computation directly. A grant of G tokens at launch prices against a float of 0.015 of supply; the insider block of 0.25 unlocks against it on the issuer's schedule. Every unlock is a dilution event for the grant-holder, and every dilution event is priced by the same 66.666667x overhang that made the headline FDV possible. The recipient's expected real income is the grant minus the insider exit schedule — which is to say, the UBI is a subordinated tranche of the insiders' liquidity event.

One is enclosure; the other is the exit.
