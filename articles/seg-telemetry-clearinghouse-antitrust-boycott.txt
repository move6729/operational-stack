### 1. The Legal Fiction of the 'Vertical' Security Filter: How 182,000 Competitors Outsourced a Group Boycott

The sender's domain is fully compliant with every open Internet engineering standard: its Sender Policy Framework (`SPF`, RFC 7208) record resolves cleanly, its DomainKeys Identified Mail (`DKIM`, RFC 6376) RSA-2048 cryptographic signature validates without error, and its Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance (`DMARC`, RFC 7489) policy enforces `p=reject`. ### 3. Inside the Reciprocal Mesh: Why Paying Members Get 96% Delivery While Authenticated Challengers Get Throttled at 12 Emails

The technical mechanics of the SEG clearinghouse expose the gulf between open Internet standards and private-equity rent extraction. Under the IETF's open mail architecture, identity and cryptographic provenance are binary, verifiable mathematical properties:
* **SPF (RFC 7208):** Publishes authorized sending IPv4/IPv6 CIDR blocks in the sender's DNS zone.
* **DKIM (RFC 6376):** Signs the message headers and body hash (`bh=`) with the sender's private key, verifiable against the public key published at `selector._domainkey.domain.tld`.
* **DMARC (RFC 7489):** Binds the visible `RFC5322.From` header domain to the authenticated SPF and DKIM domains.

When a message passes SPF, DKIM, and DMARC alignment, its cryptographic identity is non-repudiable. A genuinely unilateral, vertical spam filter executing local hygiene would evaluate the content payload and the recipient's local interaction preferences.

Instead, Proofpoint's Nexus graph and Mimecast's CyberGraph operate a **centralized cross-tenant behavioral quarantine**. The moment an external domain transmits **12 or more messages** across *any* combination of the clearinghouse's 182,000 enterprise tenants, the central telemetry hub aggregates the recipient headers (`RCPT TO`) across competing firms. Even if those 12 messages are individually tailored, one-to-one commercial proposals sent to 12 rival procurement departments in the same industry vertical, the clearinghouse treats the cross-tenant dispersion itself as a "bulk velocity anomaly."

Crucially, this velocity penalty is applied asymmetrically:
* **Out-of-Grid / Uncertified Challenger (14.0% Delivery Rate):** Any domain registered within **90 days** or operating on independent, self-hosted SMTP infrastructure without a paid certification key or incumbent SEG outbound relay faces an immediate cross-tenant throttle at 12 messages, collapsing inbox placement to **14.0%**.

### 4. The Statutory Trapdoor: Why Neither CISA (6 U.S.C. § 1507(e)) Nor Section 230(c)(2) Immunizes a Commercial Clearinghouse

When confronted with antitrust scrutiny over cross-tenant blacklisting, SEG counsel and industry trade groups reflexively invoke two federal statutory shields: the **Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015 (CISA 2015, 6 U.S.C. §§ 1501–1510)** and the Good Samaritan filtering immunity of **Communications Decency Act Section 230(c)(2) (47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(2)(A))**.

A granular reading of the statutory text and Ninth Circuit precedent reveals that both defenses collapse when applied to commercial velocity throttling and synthetic "Greymail" boycotts.

#### A. The CISA 2015 Anti-Boycott Trapdoor (`6 U.S.C. §§ 650(6), 1503(e)(1), 1507(e)`)
Section 104(e)(1) of CISA 2015 (**6 U.S.C. § 1503(e)(1)**) grants a narrow antitrust exemption for private entities that share or receive "cyber threat indicators" for the purpose of assisting with cybersecurity:

> *"Except as provided in section 1507(e) of this title, it shall not be considered a violation of any provision of the antitrust laws for 2 or more private entities to exchange or provide a cyber threat indicator, or assistance relating to the prevention, investigating, or mitigating of a cybersecurity threat, for cybersecurity purposes under this subchapter."* — **6 U.S.C. § 1503(e)(1)**.

First, this safe harbor applies *strictly* to a **"cyber threat indicator"** as defined in **6 U.S.C. § 650(6)**—namely, information necessary to describe or identify malicious reconnaissance, a method of defeating a security control, a security vulnerability, or actual unauthorized access or anomalous technical behavior indicative of a breach. A cryptographically valid (`SPF/DKIM/DMARC = PASS`), plain-text B2B sales inquiry or procurement bid from a newly incorporated vendor contains zero malware, zero exploit code, and zero unauthorized access attempts. Its sender IP, domain age, and recipient velocity across twelve prospects do not constitute a "cyber threat indicator" under **6 U.S.C. § 650(6)**; they are pure commercial transaction metadata.

Second, Congress explicitly anticipated that incumbents would attempt to disguise commercial boycotts and market allocation as "cybersecurity information sharing." In Section 108(e) of CISA 2015 (**6 U.S.C. § 1507(e)**), Congress enacted an ironclad **Anti-Boycott and Price-Fixing Carve-Out** that expressly cross-references and overrides § 1503(e)(1):

> *"Nothing in this subchapter shall be construed to permit price-fixing, allocating a market between competitors, monopolizing or attempting to monopolize a market, **boycotting**, or exchanges of price or cost information, customer lists, or information regarding future competitive planning."* — **6 U.S.C. § 1507(e)** (emphasis added).

When an SEG clearinghouse aggregates recipient lists (`customer lists` / prospective buyer headers) across 182,000 competing enterprises and executes an automated cross-tenant blockade against non-member commercial senders, it steps directly out of § 1503(e)(1) and squarely into the statutory liability trap of **6 U.S.C. § 1507(e)**.

#### B. The Section 230(c)(2) Anticompetitive Animus Exception (*Enigma Software v. Malwarebytes*)
Nor can a clearinghouse hide behind **47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(2)(A)**, which immunizes providers of interactive computer services for:

> *"any action voluntarily taken in good faith to restrict access to or availability of material that the provider or user considers to be obscene, lewd, lascivious, filthy, excessively violent, harassing, or otherwise objectionable, whether or not such material is constitutionally protected."* — **47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(2)(A)**.

In *Enigma Software Group USA, LLC v. Malwarebytes, Inc.*, 946 F.3d 1040 (9th Cir. 2019), cert. denied, 141 S. Ct. 13 (2020), a cybersecurity vendor designated a competing vendor's legitimate software programs as "Potentially Unwanted Programs" (PUPs) and quarantined them across customer machines, claiming absolute discretion under the "otherwise objectionable" clause of § 230(c)(2)(A). The Ninth Circuit rejected that defense and held that § 230(c)(2) does not confer a blank check for anticompetitive exclusion:

> *"We hold that the phrase 'otherwise objectionable' does not give a provider unbridled discretion to block online content for anticompetitive reasons...
