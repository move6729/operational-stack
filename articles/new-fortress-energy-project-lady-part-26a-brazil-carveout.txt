## III. The 24-Hour London Shell & The Synthetic Ricochet Claim (EQ-2 & EQ-3): Bypassing Absolute Priority and *Purdue Pharma* in SDNY

Having determined from the Schedule 1-A cost-benefit ledger that a UK Part 26A Restructuring Plan paired with U.S. Chapter 15 recognition dominated a plenary Chapter 11 filing by **$1,440.0 million** in preserved enterprise value—and offered a clean statutory pathway to grant legacy common shareholders a **35.0%** initial residual equity slice in exchange for a fast, uncontested handoff of the Brazilian crown jewels—the restructuring architects faced a threshold jurisdictional obstacle: **New Fortress Energy was an American company with New York-law debt and Brazilian and Caribbean physical terminals.**

To invoke the cross-class cramdown powers of Part 26A of the UK Companies Act 2006 (`§§ 901A–901G`), the plan proponents had to establish a "sufficient connection" to England and satisfy the English High Court that its judgment would have substantial international effectiveness across NFE's debt stack. Furthermore, because the Restructuring Plan compromised **$9.6 billion** of gross claims (including **$5,700.0 million** of third-party funded debt) across interlinked parent and subsidiary guarantees, the proponents needed the English court to sanction broad third-party releases extinguishing guarantee and contribution claims against non-plan subsidiaries and insiders—releases that would be per se unlawful in a domestic U.S. Chapter 11 under *Purdue Pharma*.

The mechanism engineered to bridge this jurisdictional gap was laid bare in Justice Cawson's Chancery Division sanction judgment in *Re NFE Global Holdings Limited* `[2026] EWHC 1620 (Ch)` (handed down June 26, 2026, following Justice Hildyard's convening judgment in `[2026] EWHC 1223 (Ch)`) and Chief Judge Martin Glenn's subsequent Chapter 15 recognition opinion in *In re NFE Global Holdings Limited, et al.*, Case No. 26-11268 (MG), Dkt. 30 (Bankr. ### 1. The 1-Day Co-Obligor Accession (`NFE Brazil Newco Limited`)

To manufacture an English jurisdictional anchor over the Brazilian reorganization perimeter and the New York-law notes, the corporate group incorporated a brand-new English private limited company—**`NFE Brazil Newco Limited`**—in **April 2026**. As recorded in both the English High Court judgment (`[2026] EWHC 1620 (Ch)`) and the SDNY Chapter 15 recognition decision (Case No. 26-11268 (MG), Dkt. 30), `NFE Brazil Newco Limited` executed supplemental indentures **"acceding as guarantor of the relevant notes just one day before its English proceedings commenced."**

In other words, with a lead time of exactly **1 day** (`24 hours`), a newly formed London shell with zero historical operations stepped into the shoes of a co-obligor/guarantor on billions of dollars of U.S. capital-markets debt solely to unlock the Chancery Division of the High Court of Justice in London.

### 2. Manufacturing the "Ricochet Claim" via Synthetic Deeds of Contribution

Under English restructuring jurisprudence, the High Court can release third-party claims against non-plan obligors and affiliates if leaving those claims intact would trigger "ricochet claims"—that is, if a creditor suing a non-plan guarantor would cause that guarantor to assert an indemnity or contribution claim back against the plan company, thereby unraveling the compromises inside the Part 26A plan.

Because certain NFE entities and third-party release beneficiaries did not naturally possess pre-existing contractual contribution rights that would ricochet back into the English plan companies upon a creditor lawsuit, the restructuring architects executed bespoke **Deeds of Contribution** immediately prior to launching the Part 26A proceeding. These newly drafted contracts created formal intercompany contribution obligations for the express purpose of establishing the legal predicate for third-party releases.

When reviewing this maneuver in *Re NFE Global Holdings Limited* `[2026] EWHC 1620 (Ch)`, the English High Court explicitly called out the synthetic nature of the arrangement, observing that there was **"an element of artificiality about creating a risk of ricochet effect in order to justify the inclusion of a third party release."** Nevertheless, following established English precedent permitting newly created jurisdictional and contribution hooks where commercial creditors overwhelmingly support a value-maximizing restructuring (and where no out-of-the-money creditor is left worse off than in the "relevant alternative" of an insolvency liquidation or destructive Chapter 11), the High Court sanctioned the Part 26A plan on June 26, 2026.

### 3. The SDNY Chapter 15 Comity Gate (`11 U.S.C. §§ 1506, 1507, 1521`)

Once sanctioned in London, the Part 26A Restructuring Plan still had to bind creditors and discharge New York-law debt instruments inside the United States. The Foreign Representative petitioned Chief Judge Martin Glenn in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York for recognition of the English proceedings and enforcement of the UK Restructuring Plan under **Chapter 15 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code** (`11 U.S.C. §§ 1501 et seq.`).

This presented a direct statutory collision:
- In *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, 603 U.S. 204 (2024), the U.S. Supreme Court held that `11 U.S.C. § 1123(b)(6)` does not authorize non-consensual third-party releases in a domestic **Chapter 11** reorganization plan.
- Under `11 U.S.C. § 1129(b)(2)(B)`, a domestic **Chapter 11** cramdown plan cannot allocate **35.0%** of the reorganized common equity to existing shareholders (**0.0%** permitted under strict APR) while senior classes take multi-billion-dollar haircuts.

Why did Chief Judge Glenn enter the Chapter 15 Recognition and Enforcement Order on July 14, 2026 (*In re NFE Global Holdings Limited*, Case No. 26-11268 (MG), Dkt. 30)? Because Chapter 15 operates under a fundamentally different statutory standard than Chapter 11:
1. *Purdue Pharma* construed the residual authority of `11 U.S.C. § 1123(b)(6)` within Chapter 11; it did not restrict the discretionary comity powers granted to U.S. bankruptcy courts recognizing foreign proceedings under **`11 U.S.C. § 1521(a)`** and **`§ 1507`**, where the foreign jurisdiction (England) expressly permits third-party releases to prevent ricochet claims and provides full procedural due process.
2. Under **`11 U.S.C. § 1506`**, a U.S. court may refuse to recognize a foreign judgment on public-policy grounds only if doing so would be *"manifestly contrary to the public policy of the United States"*—an exceptionally narrow exception reserved for violations of fundamental constitutional or procedural fairness, not mere differences between UK Part 26A's flexible "no worse off than the relevant alternative" rule (`Companies Act 2006 § 901G`) and the U.S. Bankruptcy Code's strict Absolute Priority Rule (`11 U.S.C. § 1129(b)(2)(B)`).
