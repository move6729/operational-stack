### I. The Folk Fallacy: Why Giving the Referee "Skin in the Game" Fixes the Match

A persistent folk aphorism circulates through contemporary discussions of institutional governance and artificial intelligence: *a machine should never be trusted to make a consequential decision because a machine cannot suffer, cannot be fined, and cannot be sent to prison.* According to this coffee-shop intuition, moral and institutional reliability require that every decision-maker possess personal biological and financial downside—popularly romanticized as "skin in the game." Because an algorithm has no body to incarcerate and no personal bank account to garnish, the folk narrative concludes that rule execution, credit adjudication, and regulatory enforcement must remain permanently vested in discretionary human officials who "feel the weight" of their decisions.

This intuition is not merely wrong; it is an exact inversion of four centuries of Anglo-American constitutional law and fifty years of formal mechanism design. Its fatal defect is a fundamental category error: **it conflates the Market Player with the Constitutional Referee.**

In any competitive economic, financial, or physical system, there are two mathematically orthogonal roles:
1. **Role A — The Principal, Capital Allocator, and Underwriter (The Players):** Entities that originate risk, allocate capital, manufacture physical or digital infrastructure, and capture residual upside.
2. **Role B — The Adjudicator, Covenant Slasher, and Rule Executor (The Referee):** The mechanism that measures state variables against pre-agreed statutory or contractual covenants and executes transfers when a boundary is breached.

When a **Player** on the field lacks personal downside exposure, the system suffers from classic agency divergence and moral hazard, as formalized by Michael Jensen and William Meckling (1976) and Kenneth Arrow (1963). If a corporate sponsor or asset manager can pocket management fees during expansions while shifting catastrophic tail losses onto public pension funds or taxpayers during contractions, the player will systematically over-leverage the enterprise. Players *must* bleed when they are wrong.

But what happens when the **Referee** has personal utility at stake in the outcome of the match? In ordinary English, a referee with personal upside or downside in the outcome of the game is called **corrupt**. A judge who receives a percentage of the fine, a credit agent whose bonus depends on waiving a sponsor's covenant default, or a regulator negotiating post-government employment with the regulated conglomerate does not become more conscientious because they are a mortal human with a personal utility function. On the contrary, their personal utility function is the exact **attack surface** through which concentrated wealth purchases asymmetric forbearance.

To demand that the adjudicator possess personal utility is to demand that the referee be bribable, coercible, and blackmailable. The entire point of an honest referee is that the referee has **zero utility** over which player wins.

### II. Players vs. Referees: Jensen and Meckling Meet Holmström's Budget-Breaker

To formalize why the Players must bear strict residual downside while the Referee must be an invariant state machine, we need not rely on literary aphorisms; we need only invoke the foundational theorems of information economics established by Leonid Hurwicz (1960, 1972), Karl Borch (1962), Kenneth Arrow (1963), Michael Jensen and William Meckling (1976), and Bengt Holmström (1982).

Consider first **Role A (The Players)**. In *Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs and Ownership Structure* (1976), Jensen and Meckling demonstrated that agency costs arise precisely to the degree that the manager or sponsor ceases to bear $100\%$ of the residual wealth effects of their decisions. When an executive or controlling shareholder holds an unhedged, long-duration equity stake on the balance sheet—exemplified by Berkshire Hathaway Inc. (`NYSE: BRK.A`, `CIK 0001067983`) retaining massive unencumbered statutory surplus inside National Indemnity Company (`NAIC 20087`) or Alphabet Inc. (`NASDAQ: GOOGL`, `CIK 0001652044`) funding frontier capital expenditure directly out of operating cash flow—the Principal's marginal utility with respect to enterprise wealth satisfies:

$$\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial W_i} > 0, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 U_i}{\partial W_i^2} \le 0$$

Every dollar of operational failure, tort liability, or capital misallocation directly reduces the Principal's net worth $W_i$. Similarly, under the Arrow-Borch optimal risk-sharing theorem (Borch 1962; Arrow 1963), when a commercial enterprise transfers catastrophic tail risk to an independent, third-party actuarial reinsurer—such as National Indemnity (`NAIC 20087`) or Arch Capital Group Ltd. (`NASDAQ: ACGL`, `CIK 0000947484`)—the reinsurer prices the treaty ex-ante and puts its own liquid statutory surplus at risk ex-post. Both the Principal (Layer 1) and the Reinsurer (Layer 2) are **Players**. Their strictly positive marginal utility for wealth is what compels rigorous ex-ante engineering, conservative leverage, and honest reserve accounting.

Now consider **Role B (The Adjudicator)**. In *Moral Hazard in Teams* (*Bell Journal of Economics*, 1982), Bengt Holmström proved a fundamental impossibility theorem: in a multi-agent system with joint production or interdependent externalities, no closed budget-balanced sharing rule among the self-interested players can simultaneously achieve Pareto-optimal Nash equilibria without introducing an external **budget-breaker** (or rule-executor) whose own payoff is decoupled from the internal surplus extraction of the contestants.

Let the Adjudicator's state space be parameterized by a personal utility vector $\theta_{\text{exec}} = (w_{\text{salary}}, w_{\text{revolving}}, c_{\text{litigation}}, c_{\text{coercion}}, e_{\text{prestige}})$, representing current compensation, post-tenure private-sector retainers, personal litigation/scandal exposure, physical or reputational coercion, and career prestige. For the Execution Layer $\mathcal{L}_{\text{exec}}$ to map an observed physical or accounting state $x \in \mathcal{X}$ onto an enforcement action $a = \mathcal{M}(x)$ without strategic distortion, the Adjudicator's utility gradient with respect to every contestant's wealth transfer $\Delta W_i$ and every personal side-channel $\theta_{\text{exec}}$ must vanish identically:

$$\nabla_{\theta_{\text{exec}}} U_{\text{exec}}(x, a) \equiv \mathbf{0}, \qquad \frac{\partial U_{\text{exec}}}{\partial \Delta W_i} \equiv 0 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$$

We term this condition the **Zero-Utility Adjudicator Invariant**. If $\partial U_{\text{exec}} / \partial \Delta W_i \neq 0$, the Adjudicator is no longer an external Holmström referee; the Adjudicator has collapsed into the game as Player $N+1$, trading enforcement forbearance for personal utility.

### III. The $12 Mayor and the $3 Million Justice: Four Hundred Years of Trying to Strip Personal Utility Out of Human Officials

Once we state the Zero-Utility Adjudicator Invariant algebraically, the entire history of Anglo-American constitutional and administrative law snaps into focus. For four hundred years, constitutional framers and common-law jurists have recognized that **human officials are structurally conflicted adjudicators** precisely because living human beings possess non-zero personal utility functions. Every landmark due-process doctrine is an attempted institutional safeguard designed to force a human official closer to $U_{\text{exec}} \equiv 0$.

1. **Coke's Common-Law Axiom (1610) & Locke's Umpire (1689):** In *Dr. Bonham's Case*, 8 Co. Rep. 114a (C.P. 1610), Sir Edward Coke struck down an organizational charter that permitted the Royal College of Physicians to sit as judges over unlicensed practitioners while retaining half of the resulting fines for the College's own treasury. Coke held that *nemo debet esse iudex in propria causa*—no person may be a judge in their own cause—because the moment the adjudicator takes a share of the penalty, adjudication becomes plunder. Seventy-nine years later, John Locke (*Second Treatise of Government*, 1689, §§ 13, 87–90) identified this exact vulnerability as the singular reason humanity exits the state of nature: in the state of nature, every man is the executor of the law of nature, and because men have "skin in the game" regarding their own disputes, passion and self-love make them partial to themselves and their friends. Civil society exists only when execution is surrendered to a "known and indifferent Judge."
2. **Article III Institutional Isolation (1787):** When the Framers drafted Article III, Section 1 of the U.S. Constitution, they did not try to give federal judges "skin in the game." They attempted the exact opposite: they granted judges life tenure during "good Behaviour" and a compensation "which shall not be diminished during their Continuance in Office." Article III, Section 1 is a **zero-utility institutional isolation mechanism**. By fixing the judge's salary against downward legislative reprisal and severing tenure from electoral whims, the Framers tried to flatten the judge's financial utility gradient ($\nabla_w U_{\text{judge}} \to \mathbf{0}$). Congress later reinforced this isolation with 28 U.S.C. § 455 (mandatory disqualification for any financial interest, however small, in a party) and 18 U.S.C. §§ 207–208 (criminalizing participation in matters affecting prospective employers or personal financial holdings).
3. **The $12 Mayor (*Tumey v. Ohio*, 1927) & Structural Municipal Capture (*Ward*, 1972):** In *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510 (1927), the Village of North College Hill paid its Mayor an extra $\$12$ court-cost fee out of the defendant's fine, but only if the Mayor convicted the defendant; an acquittal yielded $\$0$. Chief Justice William Howard Taft struck down the conviction under the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause, establishing that even a $\$12$ direct utility coupling destroys judicial neutrality. In *Ward v. Village of Monroeville*, 409 U.S. 57 (1972), the Supreme Court extended *Tumey* even where the Mayor received zero personal fee, because traffic fines funded a substantial portion of the village budget the Mayor was responsible for managing—proving that organizational and political utility corrupts the referee just as reliably as personal cash.
4. **The Class-Action Justice (*Aetna v. Lavoie*, 1986) & The $3.0 Million Campaign (*Caperton*, 2009):** In *Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Lavoie*, 475 U.S. 813 (1986), an Alabama Supreme Court justice cast the deciding vote affirming a punitive-damages doctrine against an insurer while simultaneously prosecuting his own nearly identical bad-faith lawsuit against another insurance company—using his judicial opinion to increase the settlement value of his private litigation. And in *Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co.*, 556 U.S. 868 (2009), the CEO of Massey Coal spent $\$3.0\text{ million}$ supporting the election of a West Virginia Supreme Court justice while Massey's appeal of a $\$50.0\text{ million}$ fraud verdict was pending—a campaign-expenditure-to-judgment ratio of exactly:

$$\rho_{\text{Caperton}} = \frac{\$3,000,000}{\$50,000,000} = 6.0\%$$

Once elected, the justice refused to recuse himself and cast the deciding vote reversing the $\$50.0\text{ million}$ judgment. The U.S. Supreme Court reversed, holding that when private expenditure creates a "serious, objective risk of actual bias," human psychological self-assessment of fairness is constitutionally worthless.
5. **The Death of In-House Agency Adjudication (*SEC v. Jarkesy* & *Loper Bright*, 2024):** Most recently, in *SEC v. Jarkesy*, 603 U.S. 109 (2024), and *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, 603 U.S. 369 (2024), the Supreme Court dismantled the administrative state's attempt to fuse the prosecutor, policy-writer, and referee inside the same executive bureaucracy. In *Jarkesy*, the Court held that the SEC cannot adjudicate common-law fraud penalties before its own in-house Administrative Law Judges, where the agency enjoys a structural home-court utility advantage. In *Loper Bright*, the Court terminated *Chevron* deference, holding under Section 706 of the Administrative Procedure Act that an executive agency with political and bureaucratic skin in the game may not act as the final judicial interpreter of the statutory boundaries governing its own power.

Look closely at the trajectory from 1610 to 2024. Every single constitutional crisis of adjudication arises because **a human referee cannot stop being a living human being with personal and familial interests**. You can give a human judge life tenure under Article III, and they still have children who need law-firm partnerships, spouses with consulting businesses, ideological vanity, post-retirement arbitration ambitions, and physical mortality. The human utility function leaks through every statutory seal.

### IV. The Asymmetric Revolving Door: Why Bureaucrats Never Suffer When They Fail the Public, Only When They Anger the Powerful

Proponents of human administrative discretion frequently argue that we need human regulators and discretionary bankruptcy or credit administrators because "a human official can be held accountable when things go wrong." Let us test that claim against the primary mathematics of George Stigler's *The Theory of Economic Regulation* (1971) and Mancur Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965).

Consider a human discretionary official—an agency enforcement director, a bank examiner, or an administrative referee—presiding over a binary enforcement decision $a \in \{0, 1\}$, where $a = 1$ enforces a strict covenant slash or statutory penalty and $a = 0$ grants discretionary forbearance (a waiver, an extension, or a no-action letter).

Two groups have a stake in the decision:
- **Group A (The Diffuse Public / Pension Beneficiaries / Retail Creditors):** $N_A = 10,000,000$ individuals, each with a per-capita exposure of $\$1,000$ (total public stake $W_A = \$10,000,000,000$). Because of Olson's collective-action friction, diffuse citizens cannot coordinate to offer the regulator a post-government career, nor can they credibly punish the regulator if a waived covenant blows up five years after the regulator has rotated into the private sector. At best, if the failure becomes a front-page scandal, the official faces a small probability $p_{\text{scandal}} = 0.05$ of a congressional hearing or reputational reprimand carrying a disutility equivalent to $C_{\text{scandal}} = \$200,000$—an expected public accountability cost of exactly:

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{Cost}_{\text{public}}] = p_{\text{scandal}} \cdot C_{\text{scandal}} = 0.05 \times \$200,000 = \$10,000$$

- **Group B (The Concentrated Sponsor / Financial Conglomerate):** $N_B = 1$ unified balance sheet with $W_B = \$10,000,000,000$ at stake. If the human official enforces the rule ($a = 1$), the conglomerate immediately deploys scorched-earth litigation, administrative exhaustion, and career retaliation ($\gamma_{\text{retaliation}} = 0.0005$ per dollar of contested exposure). Conversely, if the human official grants forbearance ($a = 0$) and accommodates the industry's preferred accounting structure, the official preserves eligibility for the private-sector revolving door—partner chairs at elite defense firms or senior advisory retainers at alternative asset managers yielding $w_{\text{revolving}} = \$2,500,000\text{ to }\$5,000,000$ per year against a statutory Senior Executive Service base salary of $w_{\text{gov}} = \$225,000$ ($\beta_{\text{revolving}} = 0.0020$ expected capture coupling across the career horizon).

Combining the revolving-door carrot and the retaliatory stick yields a total capture coupling slope of $\beta_{\text{revolving}} + \gamma_{\text{retaliation}} = 0.0025$. Under the Stigler-Olson Capture Inequality, the human official's utility is maximized by granting forbearance ($a = 0$) whenever the concentrated counterparty's balance-sheet stake $W_B$ exceeds the **Critical Capture Threshold** $W_{\text{capture}}^*$:

$$W_{\text{capture}}^* = \frac{p_{\text{scandal}} \cdot C_{\text{scandal}}}{\beta_{\text{revolving}} + \gamma_{\text{retaliation}}} = \frac{\$10,000}{0.0025} = \$4,000,000$$

Read that threshold again: **$\$4.0\text{ million}$.** Whenever a concentrated counterparty has more than $\$4.0\text{ million}$ at stake—and every institutional credit facility, corporate merger, or systemic regulatory audit involves hundreds of millions or billions of dollars—the human official's personal utility gradient points overwhelmingly toward accommodation. At an institutional stake of $W_B = \$10.0\text{ billion}$, the concentrated capture pressure exerted on the human official is:

$$\text{Capture Pressure}(W_B = \$10\text{B}) = 0.0025 \times \$10,000,000,000 = \$25,000,000$$

That $\$25,000,000$ career and litigation vector is **$2,500.0\times$ larger** than the $\$10,000$ expected cost of public scandal.

This is the cold mathematical reality that demolishes the folk myth of the "accountable human regulator." Human bureaucrats do not suffer when they fail the diffuse public; they suffer only when they inconvenience concentrated capital. Giving the referee a biological career, a family budget, and a post-government job market does not protect the public—it guarantees that any counterparty with more than $\$4.0\text{ million}$ at risk can buy the referee's forbearance on the installment plan.
