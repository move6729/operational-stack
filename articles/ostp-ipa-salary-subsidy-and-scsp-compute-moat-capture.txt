## 2. The Statutory Firewalls — and the Route Around Them

Congress built two firewalls against exactly this arrangement.

Firewall 1 — 18 U.S.C. § 209. The federal government's compensation for its executive employees is supposed to come from the federal government. Section 209 prohibits, criminally and civilly, the private supplementation of a federal executive salary. The purpose is elementary: a federal official whose paycheck is topped up by a private principal serves two paymasters, and the second paymaster is not accountable to the electorate.

Firewall 2 — 31 U.S.C. § 1342 (Antideficiency Act). Agencies may not accept voluntary services or fund operations outside congressional appropriations. The power of the purse belongs to Congress; a private endowment that quietly funds agency staffing has effectively amended an appropriations act without a vote.

The route around both firewalls is a detailing statute with good intentions: the Intergovernmental Personnel Act of 1970 (IPA, 5 U.S.C. §§ 3371–3376). IPA lets qualifying organizations — including tax-exempt 501(c)(3) institutions — detail personnel to federal agencies temporarily. Under an IPA arrangement, the sending organization typically keeps paying the detailee. That design feature is precisely the structural tension: when the sending organization is a private philanthropic conduit, the federal official's compensation flows from a private payroll, which is the exact configuration the § 209 firewall exists to police, and the staffing itself arrives outside the agency's appropriated headcount, which is the exact configuration the § 1342 firewall exists to police.

The third statute completes the grid: 18 U.S.C. § 208, which prohibits a federal employee — including a Special Government Employee (SGE) under 18 U.S.C. § 202 — from participating personally and substantially in a particular matter that has a direct and predictable effect on a personal financial interest. Advisory-board service is covered. Waivers exist, but a waiver is a document, and a document is either produced or it is not.

The fourth statute is the aggregation clause: 18 U.S.C. § 371, whose Hammerschmidt clause reaches conspiracies not only to steal from the United States but to impair, obstruct, or defeat any lawful government function by deceit, craft, trickery, or dishonest means. A coordinated scheme to route private payroll through a detailing statute while the agency's own ethics and appropriations controls are bypassed is the paradigm shape of that offense. Whether the coordination reaches that threshold is a question of evidence; the structural fit is not.
