## 1. The Half-Million-Dollar Machine You Are Not Allowed to Fix

Walk onto any grain farm in Illinois during the first week of October and you will find a paradox worth more than the land itself. The farmer owns a combine — title in hand, property tax paid, half a million dollars of depreciating steel parked under a shed — and yet the farmer does not control it. When a $450.00 sensor fails in the middle of the harvest window, the machine derates into limp mode, and the physical fix that any competent operator could complete in an afternoon is legally and cryptographically incomplete. The fault code will not clear. The key that clears it lives in dealer-only software — the OEM's `Service ADVISOR` diagnostic suite and its associated ECU security-access key material — and the only lawful conduit to that key is the authorized dealership network.

This is not a maintenance inconvenience. It is an aftermarket hostage structure, and in January 2025 it became the subject of a federal antitrust complaint: *FTC and States of Illinois, Minnesota, et al. v. Deere & Company*, N.D. Ill. Case No. 3:25-cv-50017, alleging Sherman Act § 2 (`15 U.S.C. § 2`) aftermarket monopolization and § 1 (`15 U.S.C. § 1`) tying, running in parallel with the consolidated private class action *In re Deere & Company Repair Services Antitrust Litigation*, N.D. Ill. MDL No. 3030.

The complaint's most damaging allegation is not that the locks exist. It is *what the locks cover*. The OEM's public defense — the null hypothesis, $\mathcal{H}_0$ — is that cryptographic ECU locks prevent EPA emissions tampering and safety hazards. But the pleadings describe lock coverage extending to seat switches, grain-tank augers, and ordinary sensor/actuator faults: components with no emissions predicate and no safety predicate whatsoever. A lock that covers a seat switch is not an emissions control. It is a tollbooth.

There is a second asymmetry hiding inside the first. The farmer paid for the machine in full — a half-million-dollar asset financed through an operating loan, depreciating on the farm's books, insured and taxed as farm property. Yet the purchase transferred only the body. The machine's fault state — the single bit that separates a working combine from a limp-mode paperweight — remained on the dealer side of a cryptographic boundary the farmer cannot cross without paying a toll. Title to the steel was sold; sovereignty over the steel was retained. That retention is what converts every ordinary breakdown into a captive transaction, and it is the precise mechanism the FTC's complaint describes when it defines a repair aftermarket with no substitute supplier.

## 2. EQ_01: The Arithmetic of a Single Fault

Strip the rhetoric and the tethering regime reduces to five locked numbers and one multiplier.

**The independent baseline.** A representative sensor/actuator fault — the kind that throws a diagnostic trouble code and derates the machine — costs $C_{indep} = \$450.00$ to fix through an independent mechanic or the farmer's own wrench: part plus labor, nothing more.

**The tethered invoice.** The same physical fault, routed through the captive dealer channel, produces an invoice of $C_{dealer} = \$2{,}250.00$: dispatch fee, diagnostic connect fee, and the software-unlock charge for keystrokes that cost the OEM nothing to execute. The pure software-dispatch markup is

$$\rho_{dispatch} = \frac{C_{dealer}}{C_{indep}} = \frac{2250.0}{450.0} = 5.0\times$$

**The temporal confiscation.** The deeper extraction is not on the invoice at all. During the weather-critical harvest window, the machine sits in the dealer queue for $D_{queue} = 4.0$ days. Every idle day, standing crop degrades — header shatter, moisture loss, yield discount — at $\ell_{daily} = \$3{,}500.00$ per day:

$$L_{downtime} = D_{queue} \cdot \ell_{daily} = 4.0 \times \$3{,}500.00 = \$14{,}000.00$$

**The combined extraction.** One $450.00 fault, fully tethered, costs the farm:

$$C_{tethered} = C_{dealer} + L_{downtime} = \$2{,}250.00 + \$14{,}000.00 = \$16{,}250.00$$

$$M_{enclosure} = \frac{C_{tethered}}{C_{indep}} = \frac{16250.0}{450.0} = 36.111111\times$$

A 36.111111x multiplier on a wrench job. And note the composition: $\$14{,}000.00$ of the $\$16{,}250.00$ — 86.2% — is pure downtime that no invoice ever itemizes. The dealer bills $2,250.00; the *lock* bills the remaining $14,000.00 directly out of the harvest.

Scale it by the fault arrival rate and the regime difference becomes a balance-sheet event. A combine working a weather-shortened harvest can plausibly throw six tethered-class faults in a season. Six faults under the sovereign regime cost $6 \times \$450.00 = \$2{,}700.00$. Six faults under the tethered regime cost $6 \times \$16{,}250.00 = \$97{,}500.00$ — a $\$94{,}800.00$ extraction differential on a single machine in a single season, before a single part price is ever marked up. The markup is not on the wrench. It is on the farmer's inability to use it.

The game-theoretic cruelty is that the farmer cannot decline to play. Fault arrival is exogenous — physics, dust, vibration, not consumer choice — and the machine *is* the harvest. Refusing the dealer invoice does not avoid the $14,000.00; it extends it. That is the definition of hostage economics: the counterparty's exit option has been engineered to zero, which is precisely why the FTC frames the dealer network's exclusive key custody as an aftermarket with no substitute supplier.

## 3. The 2023 MOU: Access Theater as the $\mathcal{H}_0$ Defense

When right-to-repair legislation advanced in statehouses, the OEM answered in January 2023 with a Memorandum of Understanding with the American Farm Bureau Federation: dealers would share manuals, diagnostic tools, and training. The MOU is the centerpiece of the $\mathcal{H}_0$ defense — *access already exists; no lock problem remains*.

The FTC's complaint alleges the opposite operational reality: the promised access is discretionary, revocable at will, and — decisively — excludes the software re-initializations and security-access key operations that actually clear limp-mode faults. Manuals describe the machine; keys command it. An MOU that delivers documentation while withholding command is not repair access. It is a reading room attached to a locked cockpit.

The private bar reached the same structure from the bottom up. *In re Deere & Company Repair Services Antitrust Litigation*, MDL No. 3030 in the same Northern District of Illinois, consolidates farmer and independent-shop class actions alleging that the software-lock regime forced overpayment for repair services in a captive aftermarket — the same two-market theory (a competitive equipment market upstream, a monopolized repair market downstream) that the FTC and the plaintiff states of Illinois and Minnesota plead under Sherman Act § 2. When the federal regulator and the consolidated private classes converge on the same aftermarket definition, the $\mathcal{H}_0$ defense bears the full burden of explaining why a seat switch needs a cryptographic guardian.

Under the *Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.*, 497 U.S. 1 (1990), disclosed-facts discipline, the $\mathcal{H}_0$ position is stated at full strength and then tested against the primary record. It fails on three independent vectors:

1. **Coverage falsification.** The pleadings' own examples — seat switches, grain-tank augers, non-emissions sensors — show lock scope untethered from any emissions or safety predicate.
2. **Statutory falsification.** The Librarian of Congress, in the `17 U.S.C. § 1201` triennial rulemakings (agricultural-equipment exemption granted in 2018, renewed in 2021 and 2024), found that circumvention of firmware locks for good-faith repair does not impair the value of the copyrighted firmware. The federal exemption process itself rejected the harm theory that underwrites $\mathcal{H}_0$.
3. **Exhaustion falsification.** *Impression Products, Inc. v. Lexmark International, Inc.*, 581 U.S. 360 (2017), holds that an authorized sale exhausts all patent rights in the sold article, and post-sale servitudes — "single-use only," "no self-repair" — are unenforceable at common law. The farmer who paid half a million dollars bought the machine, not a lease on its fault codes.

## 4. EQ_02: The Second Enclosure — Germplasm Under Patent

The silicon enclosure extracts per fault. The carbon enclosure extracts per acre, per year, forever — and it is structurally tighter, because the escape hatch is not a withheld key but an enjoined biological act.

For ten thousand years, agriculture ran on one invariant: save seed from this harvest, plant it next spring. The saved-seed baseline costs $C_{heirloom} = \$28.00$ per acre — cleaning, storage, and the labor the farm already owns. A four-firm germplasm oligopoly with concentration ratio

$$CR_4 = 0.85$$

in corn and soybean seed (the USDA Economic Research Service concentration baseline) replaced that invariant with utility patents on trait stacks, enforced through bag-tag Technology/Stewardship Agreements that every purchaser signs at the seed dealer's counter.

The Supreme Court sealed the enclosure in *Bowman v. Monsanto Co.*, 569 U.S. 278 (2013). Vernon Bowman, an Indiana farmer, bought commodity soybeans containing patented Roundup Ready traits and planted them, arguing that patent exhaustion freed the seeds he bought. The Court held unanimously that exhaustion does not extend to *making new copies* of a patented invention: planting and harvesting reproduces the claimed article, and reproduction is exactly what the patent covers. The bag you buy is a single-season planting license. The germplasm itself never sells.

Consider what that holding does to the farm's amortization schedule. Every other capital input on the farm — the tractor, the combine, the grain bin, the tile plow — is bought once and depreciated over years. Seed under the saved-seed baseline followed the same curve: buy once, replant indefinitely, amortize toward zero. *Bowman* deleted the amortization curve itself. The input that biologically reproduces — the one input whose physical nature would drive its long-run price toward marginal cost — is the one input the law now forces into perpetual repurchase. Enclosure, here, is not a price. It is the removal of the mechanism by which prices fall.

The per-acre arithmetic is locked:

$$\rho_{seed} = \frac{C_{trait}}{C_{heirloom}} = \frac{140.0}{28.0} = 5.0\times$$

$$R_{annual} = (C_{trait} - C_{heirloom}) \cdot A = (\$140.00 - \$28.00) \times 1{,}000 = \$112{,}000.00 \text{ per year, per 1,000-acre farm}$$

where $C_{trait} = \$140.00$/acre is the patented trait seed plus its tied herbicide package. The tie matters: the trait stack is engineered for the oligopoly's own chemistry, so this spring's seed purchase pre-commits this season's chemical program, and the chemical program re-commits next spring's seed purchase. The loop closes back onto the same $CR_4 = 0.85$ counterparty set on both legs.

The enforcement moment is contractual, and it happens at the counter. Every bag is sold under a bag-tag Technology/Stewardship Agreement: the grower warrants no seed saving, consents to field audits and purchase-record inspection, and accepts termination-of-access terms for replanting. The utility patent supplies the federal cause of action; the bag tag supplies the private surveillance layer; and the USDA Economic Research Service concentration baseline — $CR_4 = 0.85$ in corn and soybean seed — supplies the missing exit. For most trait stacks there is simply no non-oligopoly supplier to defect to, because the lender's yield assumptions, the elevator's expectations, and the herbicide program are all built around the trait. The farmer signs because the alternative is no trait at all. That is a tied market in biological form, and it executes with a regularity no software lock can match: every spring, on every acre, automatically.

## 5. $\mathcal{H}_0$ at Full Strength: The R&D Defense, Tested

The seed conglomerates' on-the-record defense deserves the same *Milkovich* treatment: trait royalties fund drought-tolerance and yield R&D whose benefits flow back to farmers. Stated at full strength, it is a real argument — breeding is expensive, and free germplasm under-invests.

It fails on the disclosed numbers. A competitive R&D-recovery market does not sustain $CR_4 = 0.85$ concentration with a locked $5.0\times$ per-acre markup over the public-domain baseline and a tied-chemical loop that removes the buyer's ability to substitute on either leg. Price theory assigns that signature to oligopoly rent, not to innovation recovery. And the falsification is symmetric with the equipment side: just as the Librarian of Congress found no copyright harm in repair circumvention, the exhaustion doctrine of *Impression Products* found no lawful vehicle for post-sale servitudes — the two 2017–2024 doctrinal arcs converge on the same conclusion: the enclosure's legal mortar is failing even where its economic concrete holds.

The two arcs are converging from opposite directions on the same structural point. Copyright law, through the DMCA triennial process, has already conceded that the firmware lock protects no copyright interest worth enforcing against repair. Patent law, through *Impression Products*, has already conceded that the sale of the machine exhausts every right the OEM could assert against its owner. What remains is neither copyright nor patent — it is key custody plus contract plus concentration, enforced by the simple fact that the farmer's machine is mid-field and the dealer holds the only copy of the command. Strip the legal scaffolding and the enclosure's load-bearing member is a database of keys. That is why the exit is architectural rather than legislative: open the bus, flash the firmware, save the seed, and the tollbooth has nothing left to collect from.

*Bowman* survives, but only at its exact boundary: replanting patented seed *makes a new article*. That boundary is a property of the patent claim, not of biology. Public-domain germplasm — land-grant university varieties, pre-1970 heirlooms, farmer-bred populations — carries no claim, no bag tag, and no injunction, and it restores $C_{heirloom} = \$28.00$/acre as a lawful production frontier the moment a farm chooses to walk onto it.

## 6. The Exit: Open Bus, Owner Keys, Saved Seed

The disintermediation stack requires no new physics, because the openness already exists at the physical layer:

1. **Open bus dialects.** ISO 11783 (`ISOBUS`) and SAE J1939 are published, royalty-free implement and powertrain bus standards. Every modern implement already speaks an open protocol; only the cryptographic key custody above it is proprietary. An open-bus bare-metal implement with owner-held diagnostic tooling collapses $M_{enclosure} \to 1.0\times$: the $450.00 fault costs $450.00 again, and the $14,000.00 downtime tax evaporates with the queue.
2. **Owner-flashed firmware.** Under the `17 U.S.C. § 1201` agricultural-equipment exemption, good-faith repair circumvention is federally sanctioned. Bare-metal diagnostic nodes — the sovereign farm telemetry architecture of the open autonomous agricultural protocol lineage — read codes, clear faults, and re-initialize controllers locally, at 20-watt-class power envelope, with zero dealer round-trip.
3. **Saved-seed germplasm commons.** Public-domain land-grant varieties plus legal seed saving drive $R_{annual} \to \$0.00$, and community seed libraries and cooperative breeding pools restore the ten-thousand-year amortization schedule that *Bowman* enjoined only for patented stacks.
