## I. The Fifty-Year Loan Against the Future

For half a century, the architecture of public secrecy rested on a single, unexamined wager: that a sufficiently large integer is hard to factor.

That wager was formalized in 1976 and 1977, when the Diffie-Hellman key exchange and the RSA cryptosystem converted number theory into a commercial infrastructure. It was extended in the 1990s and 2000s by elliptic-curve signatures, and by the 2010s it had become the load-bearing assumption beneath TLS, SWIFT, code signing, firmware attestation, diplomatic cable traffic, and the entire archival apparatus of the administrative state. The wager was not merely cryptographic. It was *temporal*. Every encrypted record was a loan against the future: the record was protected today on the promise that the future would not possess the machine required to open it.

`Shor 1994` ended the promise. A fault-tolerant quantum computer factors integers and computes discrete logarithms in $O((\log N)^3)$ time. The constant is large, the engineering is hard, and the date is uncertain — but the asymptotic is not in dispute. The date has a name, `Y2Q`, and the interval between the present and that date is the only variable that matters.

The second half of the old regime was not cryptographic at all. Opacity was preserved by **un-indexed fragmentation**. Records existed in public dockets, technical report libraries, committee transcripts, and regulatory filings, but the cost of locating and cross-linking them exceeded any adversary's budget. Availability was nominally one and effectively near zero. The opacity rent was carried by two terms: ciphertext secrecy and retrieval friction.

Both terms are now collapsing at the same time. `HNDL` — harvest now, decrypt later — means that the ciphertext of the 1995–2025 interval is already in adversarial vaults, waiting for the machine. Edge replication, continuous docketing, and commodity search mean that retrieval friction is approaching zero. The two-term rent is going to zero, and the actors who live on that rent have to find a third term.

They found it. It is integrity.

## II. The Statutory Order Inversion

`44 U.S.C. § 3552(b)(3)` defines information security as protecting information and information systems from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction in order to provide **integrity**, **confidentiality**, and **availability**. The statute enumerates the triad in the order $I \to C \to A$.

The operational doctrine of 1976–2026 inverted that order into $C \to I \to A$. Confidentiality became the primary property, integrity became a derived property of the ciphertext, and availability became an infrastructure concern delegated to the storage layer. The inversion was coherent only under the public-key hardness assumption. Once confidentiality is purchased by a mathematical wager, integrity is whatever the ciphertext says it is, and availability is whatever the storage vendor guarantees.

The inversion had a second consequence that was invisible for fifty years. It made integrity *dependent* on confidentiality. If the ciphertext could not be read, its integrity was protected by the same wall. The wall was doing double duty, and nobody had to price the second duty separately.

When the wall falls, the second duty is exposed. Integrity is no longer protected by confidentiality, because confidentiality is gone. Integrity becomes an independent attack surface, and it becomes the only remaining surface on which opacity rent can be charged.
